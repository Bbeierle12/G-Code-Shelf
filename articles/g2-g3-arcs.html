G2 and G3 Arcs

When your slicer writes a curve, it writes the toolpath, the sequence of moves the print head makes, as hundreds of tiny straight lines. G2 and G3 are the one-line alternative: single commands that say “sweep an arc,” clockwise or counter-clockwise.

The way to learn them is to read a line the way a parser does. Marlin’s own reference gives this example: G2 X125 Y32 I10.5 J10.5. G2 means the arc runs clockwise. The start is never written; it is always wherever the print head already is. X125 Y32 is where the arc ends. I10.5 J10.5 places the circle’s center, measured from the start rather than from the corner of the bed. That anchoring is the most common misread of an arc line, and now you will not make it.

Two more forms finish the picture. G2 I20 J20 has no destination at all, which means a full clockwise circle around a center offset (20, 20) from the start; the omission is the notation, and the head comes back to where it began. And one arc can be written two ways: G3 X2 Y7 I-4 J-3 and G3 X2 Y7 R5 are the same counter-clockwise sweep, first with center offsets, then with a radius. R is the radius shortcut, with rules: X or Y is required, and R never mixes with I and J. These lines can carry an E value too, which on your machine reads under the M83 rule from the earlier letter. Per-move amounts, nothing new to learn.

The slicer’s half of the story is a toggle. OrcaSlicer’s Arc fitting, under Quality then Precision with the mode selector on Advanced or Expert, rewrites long runs of short G1 moves into a smaller number of G2 and G3 commands. The OrcaSlicer docs are admirably honest about what it buys you: smaller files that upload faster, less wear on an SD card, and fewer moves for the motion planner to process. Print quality itself is untouched, since the geometry never changes, only its notation. Yesterday’s letter taught you that the controller queues and plans moves ahead, which is exactly why how a curve gets written into the file is the controller’s business.

But the slicer only proposes, and the firmware disposes, and the three families disagree. Marlin supports arcs, yet even Marlin turns them back into short straight segments internally, about a millimeter each, before anything moves. So even where arcs “work,” the machine still flies segments; G2 is a compact way of saying them. Klipper supports G2 and G3 only when a config section called [gcode_arcs] is enabled. I am flagging this one deliberately, because two community OrcaSlicer docs contradicted each other on it and Klipper’s own documentation settled the question. That is the verify-everything rule you hold the Instagram account to, doing its work in the wild. Bambu is the open question: I found no official answer. The community evidence is a forum thread arguing that modern smoothing planners compute their best motion from straight segments and that arcs get in the way, while Bambu Studio itself warns to disable arc fitting even as it leaves the toggle on by default, on the H2S at least.

So I will not print a claim about your A1 Mini that I cannot support. Here is the one check I would hand you instead. Slice a small cylinder for the A1 Mini, export the G-code, and search the file for G2 and G3. If the count is zero, your own toolchain has told you how it writes curves. Then open the A1 Mini profile, find the Arc fitting toggle, and note its state. Send both back to me and I will tell you what your evidence means.

We have met arcs once before, as a line item: ArcWelderLib sat in the very first briefing, back on October 15. It took a week of G1 moves, the M83 rule, and the planner queue to be ready to actually read one. And if a follower ever asks why a vase wall printed faceted, you will know the answer lives here: the slicer wrote the curve as segments, and Resolution and arc fitting are the two knobs that change how the curve is written. Same plastic path, different notation.
