Feedrate and the Speed Stack

A line that says F3000 does not promise a print head moving at 50 millimetres a second. It sets 50 as the top speed the planner will try to reach, and on a short, detailed print it may never arrive.

Feedrate is the requested speed of a move, in millimetres per minute, and once set it sticks until another F replaces it. So G1 X10 Y20 E5 F3000, the line from the G-code reel, asks for a move to that point, extruding 5 millimetres on the way, at 3000 millimetres per minute, or 50 a second. The head still has to speed up and slow down again, so its speed rises and falls rather than holding flat at the requested number.

A reset block quoted for Bambu machines in a forum thread carries M204 S10000, in Bambu's older S form, setting how quickly speed itself can change, at 10,000 millimetres per second squared. That is a community-quoted example, not Bambu documentation, and I have not confirmed it in your own start file yet. With that quoted value, 50 millimetres a second arrives after just 0.125 millimetres of travel from rest. But a detailed print is mostly short segments and corners, and on a segment half a millimetre long the head spends the whole trip speeding up and slowing down, then brakes again for the corner.

Marlin calls that corner rule jerk, or in its newer form junction deviation, while Klipper calls it square corner velocity, with a default of 5 millimetres a second at a right-angle corner. Different names, same job. M201 caps acceleration per axis, while M204 caps it by move type, and both apply. I found no matching corner line for your A1 Mini, so that figure is one I am leaving blank for now. The hot end's melting limit caps how much plastic can flow, whatever the mechanics allow.

Then come the two knobs that look alike and do different jobs. M220 scales head speed, while M221 scales only how much plastic comes out. That same quoted reset block sets both to 100, which is the safest way to hold them in mind: two independent percentages. If a print runs slow for no visible reason, I would check M220 first, and if its lines come out thin and underfed, I would check M221. Both definitions are in Prusa's firmware reference.

The one check worth doing is in your own files: open a recent slice and find its M204 line and its M220 and M221 lines, to see what your profile actually wrote. After that, a slow print stops being a mystery and becomes a question with four possible answers: the ceiling, the ramp, the corner, or the melt limit. I will keep an eye out for anything that changes this.
