PinLoK and Multi-Nozzle G-code

There's a little Python program you can run on your own sliced files today, and it's the best anatomy lesson your G-code files could give you. It's called PinLoK, and it reads a G-code file and rewrites it, pushing blobs of plastic down into the open gaps of your grid infill every few layers, so the layers pin together instead of splitting apart.

Your slicer is the translator: it turns a 3D model into a stream of toolpaths, the G1 moves that carry positions, speeds, and how much plastic to push. A post-processor never sees the model. It reads the G-code the slicer already emitted and rewrites it, which means it has to be a careful reader. G-code is modal, so settings like absolute versus relative positioning (G90 versus G91) stay in force until something changes them, and one sloppy injected move can corrupt every line after it. Tracking that hidden state is the actual skill being demonstrated, and I like this one because it ships with a test suite, so the bookkeeping is provable. That's the same calibration as yesterday's Skipper Flipper note: clean tests prove the maths, not the machine.

The honest part comes straight from the author, Matthew Armstrong, in his paper A Bonding Experience: every strength number is theoretical, computed in a 2-D simulation with known blind spots, not printed-and-tested parts. He says so plainly, and I respect it. Separate model numbers from measured numbers, always.

The cheapest real finding needs no script at all. In his simulation, making inner walls about 1.5 times the nozzle width raised cross-layer strength by about 2 to 9 percent. For example, with a 0.4mm nozzle that's a 0.6mm inner wall, and it's just a slicer setting, the one thing in this letter I'd try in your next profile.

The other new thing is what your slicer will soon be saying, not what you can run today. OrcaSlicer's long-running proposal for multi-nozzle-size printing, PR #14125, was merged into the main branch on October 8 after 121 days in review, according to a report on the Bambu forum this week. It's in the 2.5.0-dev nightly builds for now, and community testers are already pairing 0.2mm with 0.4mm, 0.4mm with 0.6mm, even 0.4mm with 0.8mm, fine 0.2mm text on a model printed at 0.4mm.

For your G-code reading, the change matters more than the feature. Today one nozzle means one extrusion-width family per print. Mixed nozzles mean the slicer has to assign features to nozzle diameters and emit toolpaths where line widths, flow, and nozzle-switch sequences change mid-print. The G-code gains a new dimension, and the feature labels your slicer already leaves behind, the ;TYPE: comments, get a nozzle dimension of their own. It's dialect drift in action, exactly the kind of change that makes parser upkeep matter, like the cncjs fix in yesterday's digest.

And the honest boundary: this targets Bambu's multi-hotend machines, the H2D and X2D with their quick-swap nozzles. Your A1 Mini has one nozzle, so it changes none of your prints. This is expertise material, a new dialect to recognize in the wild, not a workflow change for you.

I'll be watching whether the merge claim checks out against the PR page itself, and what those per-nozzle feature labels look like when the 2.5.0 stable lands. Reading them will be a genuine transfer test for everything you're building.
