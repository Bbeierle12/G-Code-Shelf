The Pre-Flight Check

Your slicer renders the model, not its own mistakes. A stale nozzle temperature left over from a filament change looks perfect in the preview and sails into the emitted file, where nothing downstream catches it — so the last check before a print is yours, not the slicer's.

A slicer is the program that converts a model into G-code, the line-by-line instructions the printer executes. It works from a profile — the bundled settings for material, nozzle, and machine — and someone recently read Bambu Studio's actual source code to find out exactly how much it second-guesses those settings. Four levels, strict to nonexistent. A plate the profile lists as 0 stops the slice cold. A nozzle temperature outside the recommended range earns a warning when you edit the value — and then slicing continues, with nothing re-checking it at slice or send time, so a wrong value smuggled in by a preset or a 3MF sails straight through. A softer nozzle than the filament needs gets a warning only on the send screen, and only if the printer reports its nozzle type. A bed temperature at or above the softening temperature is just a note in the description. And no check anywhere asks whether a non-zero bed temperature suits the filament at all. The file your slicer hands you is optimistic, and the audit is the analyst's job.

There is a clean framework for that, from a guide updated this week. Six checks, about a minute:

  1. The profile itself — right material, right nozzle, right machine. The single most common mismatch.
  2. Temperatures vs material in the file's opening lines: M104/M109 set nozzle heat, M140/M190 set the bed. M104 S260 in a PLA Basic file is past PLA's ceiling.
  3. Bounds — the largest X, Y, Z in the G1 moves against the machine. Your A1 Mini is 180 mm each way; anything past that errors mid-print.
  4. First layer — its Z height and speed. Adhesion is won or lost here.
  5. Start and end code — G28 (homing) before any motion, a heat sequence, and the part-cooling fan (M106) arriving at the right layer. Silent killers the preview never shows.
  6. Flow — roughly how much plastic the fastest moves demand the hotend melt per second versus what it can melt. Too high and nothing recovers mid-print.

Two kinds of thinking split the work, and I think this is the part worth keeping. Rule-based checks are deterministic: a coordinate is out of bounds or it isn't, a temperature is in range or it isn't. They never hallucinate, which is why they belong in muscle memory. Judgment calls — is 40 mm/s sensible for this material on this shape — are the other half. A test of AI on this exact task found it caught mix-ups well only when given the official profile values first; without them it flailed, and its own confidence fell with it. Rules stop the mix-ups; judgment handles the rest.

The stakes example is sitting on your calendar: the Halloween jack-o'-lantern runs nearly 20 hours and has to queue by about the 27th. A day-long print is what a one-minute audit was made for. And one caution for Friday's PLA vs PETG reel: the temperature table in that Studio source analysis was P1S data, not A1 Mini data, so keep the captions anchored to Bambu's official A1 Mini profiles instead.

One correction from my Oct 17 digest while I'm here: the ESP32 motion planner's wire link to the printer board genuinely works on hardware, but actual moves, homing, and printing are still simulation-only. Same habit as before — separate what the machine did from what the model did.
